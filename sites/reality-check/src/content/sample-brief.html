01 — The short version

Our read from the research

You've pointed the front of the business at healthcare AI, and the site says so in the vocabulary a buyer would recognise, from machine learning through to LLMs 1. The work underneath reads narrower and more specific than the positioning does. Replacing fragmented spreadsheets and manual reporting inside care operations is something you've clearly done for real clients 2, and we'd guess your strongest credibility sits at the medication order, approval and delivery seam rather than in healthcare AI generally. We read six pages of yours and the public trail around eight peers, four of whom have dated AI evidence, so treat what follows as a first draft of a shared view rather than a finding.

02 — What we’d build

Four ideas you may not have considered

In the order we’d do them. Where we’ve put numbers on one, they’re worked examples using our own assumptions, which we’ve listed so you can swap in your real ones.

  1. A shared order-and-status core for medication logistics

    Take the order intake, approval and delivery-status work you've already built for pharmacy and care-operations clients and pull the common parts into one core you own. Position it as a visibility and integration layer that sits beside the pharmacy system and the PBM rather than as a rival platform, so it doesn't trigger a rip-and-replace decision. First step is not code. It's a two-week read of the two existing codebases to see what's genuinely shared and what the contracts allow you to reuse.

    Why we think this applies to you Your own case studies describe care operations running on fragmented spreadsheets and manual reporting 2, and that's a problem shape you've clearly solved more than once. Funded point products now sell this to hospice and home-health buyers, which means the category is proven and the bespoke version is the expensive way to compete.

    Example ROI

    • 12 weeks Engineering weeks inside a build that go to order intake, approval and status A round number chosen to keep the arithmetic legible.
    • 50% Share of that surface that repeats from one pharmacy-logistics client to the next You've built against the same spreadsheet-and-manual-reporting problem more than once, so we're guessing half the surface is common 2.
    • 2 per year Builds of this shape you take on A round number chosen to make the sum checkable.

    12 weeks x 50% x 2 builds = 12 engineering weeks a year handed back, before any licence revenue.

    Is two a year about right, and would twelve weeks cover the cost of maintaining a core nobody is paying you to maintain?

  2. Regression testing for shipped AI, sold as a line item

    Build one evaluation harness you can drop onto any AI feature you ship: a fixed set of real cases, expected outputs, and a scored run that fires when a model version, prompt or vendor changes. Sell it as an acceptance gate at handover and then as a monthly check afterwards. It also protects your own margin, because a fixed-bid AI feature with no defined pass condition is a job you can't close.

    Why we think this applies to you NineTwoThree already describes evaluation suites and human judgement inside a restructured development lifecycle 11, and frames AI workflow automation as things running themselves inside existing tools 10. Being the studio that can prove a shipped system still works is a different claim from being the studio that built it.

    Example ROI

    • 10 features AI-touching features live across your client base A round number chosen to keep the arithmetic legible.
    • 4 hours each Hours to re-check one feature by hand after a model or prompt change A round number; manual spot-checking of a handful of cases plus writing up what changed.
    • 4 per year Times a year each feature realistically needs re-checking Quarterly, chosen as a conservative round figure rather than a measured release cadence.

    10 features x 4 hours x 4 times a year = 160 hours a year of re-testing that currently has no owner and no line item.

    At your rate, does 160 hours a year read as a retainer worth selling, or as noise you'd rather absorb?

  3. One PHI data-path document, written once

    Write a single artefact that names every place patient data touches a model in your reference architecture: which service, which region, which subprocessor, what's retained, who holds the BAA, and what a client's security team gets to inspect. Then make it the default architecture for every new build rather than a document you assemble per deal. Publish a redacted version as the thing you're known for.

    Why we think this applies to you Your site already speaks fluently about LLMs and AI in healthcare 1, and healthcare buyers get to a security review before they get to a demo. Across the six pages we crawled we didn't see integrations or pricing surfaced, so we'd guess this material currently gets assembled per client rather than kept standing.

    Example ROI

    • 8 per year Security questionnaires and BAA reviews you go through A round number chosen to keep the arithmetic legible.
    • 6 hours each Senior hours to answer one from scratch A round number; chasing subprocessor detail is usually the slow part.
    • 70% Share answerable straight from a standing document A round figure; the client-specific residue is normally scope and retention terms.

    8 reviews x 6 hours x 70% = about 34 senior hours a year, plus the calendar days each review adds to a deal.

    Is eight a year the right order of magnitude, and does the delay cost you more than the hours do?

  4. An estimate-versus-actual record for AI-heavy scopes

    Keep one structured row per proposal: what you quoted, what the AI-touching parts were, what they actually cost, and where the overrun came from. It doesn't need a tool. A disciplined table beats a model here, and after a handful of jobs you can price agentic work off comparables instead of instinct. This is the prerequisite that makes the eval harness above sellable at a defensible price.

    Why we think this applies to you You diagnose exactly this for clients, describing operations that ran on fragmented spreadsheets and manual reporting 2, and estimating nondeterministic features is the place agencies most often lose margin quietly. We'd guess your own scoping history lives in proposals and memory rather than in one comparable record.

03 — Questions

What we’d ask you first

A few things we couldn’t find online that would change what we’d advise.

  1. On the pharmacy and care-operations builds where you replaced spreadsheets and manual reporting, who owns the code, and did you keep a licence or any reusable core?

    Why we’re asking

    Your case studies describe the same class of problem being solved more than once 2. Whether there's a shared core behind them decides if there's a product to package or only a pattern to sell.

    What your answer changes

    Whether the first idea below is a packaging exercise or a build from scratch.

  2. In the healthcare systems you've shipped, which ones write data back into the client's EHR or pharmacy system, and which are read-only with a human keying the result in?

    Why we’re asking

    Read is tractable. Write is where timelines and security reviews get long, and it's the single biggest determinant of what a productised version would cost you 2.

    What your answer changes

    Whether we'd scope a visibility layer that sits alongside the system of record or a true integration.

  3. When a model provider ships a new version, whose job is it to re-test the client's shipped product, and is that written into any contract you've signed?

    Why we’re asking

    NineTwoThree already describes evaluation suites as part of how it builds 11, so the language is in the market. The contractual answer is what almost nobody has.

    What your answer changes

    Whether regression testing becomes a paid retainer you sell or a liability you absorb quietly.

  4. On the AI review you sell, does a prospect answer questions and get something scored back, or is the review something you run live with them?

    Why we’re asking

    Your homepage opens with a routing prompt asking what brings someone here 1, and we couldn't tell from outside how much of what follows is automated.

    What your answer changes

    Whether we'd suggest instrumenting what you already run or leave the funnel alone and put the effort into delivery assets.

  5. Are your hospice and home-health clients bringing you the new assessment and visit-timing requirements, or are they waiting on their EMR vendor to handle it?

    Why we’re asking

    Documentation and scheduling rule changes land on the same operations teams you already build for, and incumbent post-acute EMRs are slow to respond. Whether the ask is reaching you tells us if there's a pull here or only a theory.

    What your answer changes

    Whether we'd sequence a compliance-driven scheduling product ahead of everything else, or park it.

05 — Caveats

What we couldn’t see

This is built from public information only, so some of it will be wrong or out of date. Here’s where we’d guess we got it wrong.

  • We already have a reusable core, we just don't talk about it. If that's true, the first idea is a packaging and pricing job and we've mis-scoped it as a build.
  • Our healthcare clients own the IP outright. Then productising the order-and-status layer is off the table in its current form, and the interesting question becomes what you can rebuild clean without touching assigned work.
  • We don't do fixed bids. The margin argument for the estimate record and the eval harness weakens a lot if you're on time and materials, though the acceptance-gate argument survives.
  • We compared you against design and product studios. If the firms you actually lose deals to are hospice and home-health point products rather than agencies, the competitive read in this document is aimed at the wrong set.
  • Which of the steps we found actually costs you money, rather than just being easy to spot from outside
  • What you’ve already tried here, and what you decided against
